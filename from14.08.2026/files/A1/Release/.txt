# Comparing and Totally Ordering Mathematical Expressions: Is the Traditional Process Redundant?

Hung Dinh Phu Dang

## Abstract 

I call this the "classic processing workflow":

$$\text{Expressions} \xrightarrow{\text{Arithmetic Computation}} \text{Scalar Values} \xrightarrow{\text{Comparison}} \text{Relational Structure and "Spacing between expressions"
} (<, >, =)$$

Observing the center: In many applications, the final scalar value is not what is truly needed; rather, what matters is the relative ordering or distance between expressions. Here, the scalar value merely serves as an intermediate "carrier" for that relational information.
Is the intermediate step of “deriving a scalar value” a mathematically ontological necessity for transitioning from an expression to an ordering or distance relationship? Or does there exist an alternative mathematical space that allows for the extraction of such a relationship directly from the expression's structure, thereby bypassing the scalar derivation step—either partially or entirely?
This is not a proposal to eliminate computation. The goal is to redefine the concept of computation to encompass structural comparisons that do not necessarily pass through intermediate scalar values.

## 1. Definition

**Axiom A1 — Comparability**

There exists a relational operator $\preceq_{\mathbb{S}}$, defined directly on the structure (graph/syntax tree) of two elements $A, B \in \mathbb{S}$, such that:

$$\preceq_{\mathbb{S}} (A, B) \iff \operatorname{eval}(A) \le \operatorname{eval}(B)$$

In other words, $\preceq_{\mathbb{S}}$ is logically equivalent to the standard ordering relations $\{<, >, =\}$ on scalar values—but the left-hand side does not involve the explicit, full computation of $\operatorname{eval}(A)$ and $\operatorname{eval}(B)$.

**Axiom A2 — Structural Distance / Gap Measure**

There exists a function

$$d_{\mathbb{S}} : \mathbb{S} \times \mathbb{S} \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$$

(or an equivalent ordering structure), defined directly on the expression graph, such that $d_{\mathbb{S}}(A, B)$ accurately reflects the difference $\vert{}\operatorname{eval}(A) - \operatorname{eval}(B)\vert{}$ (or a monotonic transformation thereof) without requiring the full computation of $\operatorname{eval}(A)$ and $\operatorname{eval}(B)$.

**Axiom A3 — Total Order: sufficiently strong, yet without inheriting the costs associated with $\mathbb{R}$.**

$\mathbb{S}$ must support a total ordering relation with expressive power equivalent to that of $\mathbb{R}$ (sufficient to distinguish every pair of distinct finite-precision values ​​required by the problem).
To clarify the constraint of “avoiding the problems associated with $\mathbb{R}$”: this is not a constraint regarding the nature of the set itself (such as cardinality, countability, or the finite representability of $\mathbb{R}$ on computers—an issue effectively resolved in practice by finite-precision floating-point arithmetic). The real constraint lies in the processing cost—COST_ALL (defined in Section 3): if $\mathbb{S}$ is simply forced to reduce to $\mathbb{R}$ before a comparison can be made, it reverts exactly to the “classical processing workflow” that the problem aims to avoid, rendering the initial objective cost-ineffective—not “set-theoretically impossible,” but rather “cost-wise trivial or futile.”
Therefore, A3 is understood as:
$\mathbb{S}$ must possess a total order with a discriminative power equivalent to that of $\mathbb{R}$, while the process of extracting order or distance information (A1, A2) must incur a COST_ALL that is genuinely lower than that of the "reduce to $\mathbb{R}$ and then compare" approach.

**Additional condition — contextualizing costs**
A1 and A2 must be feasible within a “real-world environment and specified equipment”—that is, subject to specific hardware or computational model constraints, rather than within an idealized computational model with infinite resources. The “total actual implementation cost” (COST_ALL, Section 3) serves as the metric for feasibility—not merely theoretical feasibility (where existence alone suffices), but practical feasibility (where COST_ALL shows a genuine reduction compared to the baseline).

## 2. Two major issues

**Major Issue 1 — The General Case**

Given: $N$ expressions, each containing only basic arithmetic operations $\{+, -, \times, \div\}$ with finite-precision real number operands.
Exclusion: All transcendental functions. According to Richardson’s Theorem (1968), determining whether $\operatorname{eval}(A) = 0$ is undecidable in the general case when expressions are permitted to contain transcendental functions; thus, this exclusion is a necessary condition for the problem to remain decidable, rather than an arbitrary choice by the author.
Open question: Does there exist a system $\mathbb{S}$—along with algorithms to compute $\preceq_{\mathbb{S}}$ and $d_{\mathbb{S}}$ satisfying axioms A1–A3 for this finite-precision expression class $\{+, -, \times, \div\}$—that achieves a lower COST_ALL than the baseline?

**Major Issue 2 — The Inner Product Case**

Given: $N$ expressions of a specific (non-arbitrary) form:

$$\langle u, v \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_i$$

where $u_i$ and $v_i$ are finite-precision real numbers.

Open question: Given the stricter structural constraints (a fixed sum-of-products form rather than an arbitrary expression), are problems A1–A3 easier to solve than Major Problem 1? In other words, does the specific sum-of-products structure allow for a feasible $\mathbb{S}$ that might not exist in the general case?

## 3. Định nghĩa COST_ALL

$$\text{COST\_ALL} = \text{Cost}_{\text{computation}} + \text{Cost}_{\text{memory/representation}} + \text{Cost}_{\text{khác}}$$

measured in a real-world environment using specific hardware—not in a computational model that completely abstracts away the resources.

**Illustration via the Unary vs. Binary example**

(This is an example from the border)

Unary system: represents a value $V$ as a string of $V$ characters; for example, $i + j \to \underbrace{11\dots 1}_{i+j \text{ characters}}$
In this system, Comparability and Distance (A1, A2) become direct comparisons of length or shape, eliminating the need for mechanical addition—arithmetically, $\text{Cost}_{\text{computation}} \to O(1)$. However, $\text{Cost}_{\text{memory}} = O(V)$: for $C = 1000 \times 1000$, the Unary system requires a sequence of $10^6$ characters—entailing significant memory and unrolling costs.
Scalar/Binary system: $\text{Cost}_{\text{memory}} \approx O(\log V)$ (approximately 20 bits for a value of $10^6$), but there is a $\text{Cost}_{\text{computation}}$ associated with the step of reducing the value to a scalar before comparison and distance determination are possible.
Observation: These two systems lie at opposite ends of a computation-cost vs. memory-cost trade-off axis within a one-dimensional (unary) representation space.

## 4. Statement of the Central Open Problem

Does there exist a space $\mathbb{S}$ satisfying A1, A2, and A3 such that the COST_ALL (measured within a clearly defined computational model or physical device) required to extract the ordering relationship and distance between expressions in $\mathbb{S}$ is strictly less than the COST_ALL of the classical procedure (reducing to scalars and then comparing)—for (a) the class of general arithmetic expressions $\{+, -, \times, \div\}$ with finite precision (Major Problem 1), and (b) the class of inner product expressions $\langle u, v \rangle$ (Major Problem 2)?

**4.1 Why this is an open problem—not an impossibility**

(i) There is no proof of general impossibility. No theorem—including Richardson’s Theorem, which merely excludes transcendental functions from the scope of the problem—demonstrates the non-existence of an $\mathbb{S}$ satisfying A1–A3 with a COST_ALL lower than the baseline.
Richardson’s Theorem limits the scope of the problem (excluding transcendental functions to preserve decidability) but does not limit the existence of $\mathbb{S}$ within the remaining scope.

**(ii) There is partial precedent—evidence indicating that the non-trivial problem is not impossible:**

Regarding Major Issue 1: techniques for exact or certified geometric predicate evaluation (such as interval arithmetic and adaptive-precision arithmetic—exemplified by the work of Shewchuk and Yap in computational geometry) achieve requirement A1 (determining the sign or order of an expression) at a lower average cost than fully reducing the expression to an exact scalar value, although these techniques do not necessarily satisfy all three axioms (A1–A3) in every case.
Regarding Major Issue 2: techniques such as Locality-Sensitive Hashing, random projection (Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma), and Maximum Inner Product Search (MIPS) achieve approximations of A1/A2 for the inner product problem, with a COST_ALL lower than the baseline across various real-world data scenarios. However, as these are probabilistic or approximate solutions that do not satisfy A3 (requiring an exact total order without error), the question of whether an exact (non-approximate) solution exists remains open.
**(iii) The Unary/Binary example merely illustrates a known two-dimensional trade-off; it neither proves nor disproves the existence of the multi-dimensional intersection point sought by the central problem.** Extending the concept from a one-dimensional trade-off (unary versus binary operations on natural numbers) to a multi-dimensional structural space—generalized for arithmetic expressions or inner products—constitutes the unresolved core of Major Problems 1 and 2.
