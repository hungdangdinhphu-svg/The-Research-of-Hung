# Comparing and Totally Ordering Mathematical Expressions: Are Traditional Processing Methods Still Suitable Today?

Hung Dinh Phu Dang

*(Revised and formalized version)*

## Abstract

This paper asks whether the "classic processing workflow" — expression → scalar value in $\mathbb{R}$ → comparison — is an ontological necessity, or merely the historically dominant, possibly wasteful route to what we actually want: an expression's *position in a total order*. The original version of this paper posed this question through three informal requirements (A1–A3). This revised version replaces the informal requirements with an explicit computational model in which every claim of the form "$\mathbb{S}$ is cheaper than the classical baseline" is falsifiable, and proves what can honestly be proven within that model:

- **Proposition 1** — the exact rational value of a $\{+,-,\times,\div\}$-expression can require bit-length *exponential* in its division-depth (via continued-fraction convergents), so the "classical baseline" is not merely $O(L)$; it can be far worse.
- **Theorem 1** — a matching adversary lower bound: no algorithm, in the worst case, can determine $\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{eval}(T))$ without reading essentially all input bits. This shows the question is only interesting *relative to a baseline that is more expensive than "read the input,"* which Proposition 1 shows is generically the case.
- **Theorem 2** — the adaptive-precision / floating-point-filter method (Shewchuk 1997; Burnikel–Fleischer–Mehlhorn–Schirra) *does* satisfy A1 for Main Problem 1, at cost $O(L)$, on any instance that is not an exact or near-exact tie — restated here inside the COST_ALL framework, with an explicit statement of exactly where it degrades to the baseline.
- **Theorem 3** — a sketch-and-certify comparator for Main Problem 2 (inner products), which is a genuine win **only in the amortized, repeated-query regime** (one corpus, many queries) — a point the original draft did not make explicit, and which turns out to be essential to why LSH/MIPS methods work at all.

What remains genuinely open, now stated precisely rather than by appeal to "difficulty," is whether the **adversarial near-tie case** can be beaten *unconditionally*. Theorem 1 says no. Sections 6–7 discuss why, and correct two mathematical gaps in the original candidate constructions (tropical geometry has no native subtraction; comparing "operators instead of scalars" is a classical, not inherently quantum, idea via Weyl's inequality and randomized numerical linear algebra).

## 1. Introduction

For millennia, mathematics has rested on a pipeline: an expression is evaluated to a scalar — a point on $\mathbb{R}$ — and only then compared. This paper calls that pipeline the **classic processing workflow** and asks whether the intermediate scalar is necessary, or whether an alternative space $\mathbb{S}$ can extract an expression's position in a total order directly from its structure, at genuinely lower cost.

Section 2 gives a computational model precise enough that "cheaper" has units. Section 3 states the two main open problems. Section 4 proves what can be proven: one lower bound and two conditional upper bounds, unified by a single recurring pattern (approximate cheaply, certify only when necessary). Section 5 reassesses which parts of the original open-problem classification are now resolved. Section 6 repairs two mathematical gaps in the original speculative constructions. Section 7 is a limitations section, included deliberately: several of the strongest-sounding claims in the literature this paper draws on (tropical geometry, sketching, quantum comparison) are easy to overstate, and this section says plainly where the present paper does and does not have a proof.

## 2. Formal Model

### 2.1 Computational model

All costs are measured in the **word-RAM model with an explicit bit-probe sub-model for lower bounds**, augmented with an I/O term in the style of Aggarwal–Vitter (1988):

- A machine word holds $w$ bits ($w = 64$ in practice); arithmetic/logic on a word costs $O(1)$.
- A **bit-probe** is a single-bit read of the input representation; the bit-probe model is used *only* for lower bounds (Theorem 1), since it is the weakest possible adversary model — any lower bound proven there holds a fortiori in the word-RAM model.
- Memory is organized in blocks of $B$ words; a transfer of one block between levels of the memory hierarchy costs $O(1)$ (the I/O model), capturing bandwidth cost.

### 2.2 Expressions and exact semantics

Fix a finite-precision base representation $\mathbb{Q}_b := \{\, p/2^b : p \in \mathbb{Z},\, |p| < 2^{b+1} \,\}$ — the dyadic rationals representable in $b$ bits, i.e., IEEE‑754‑like or fixed-point leaves. An **expression tree** $T$ has leaves in $\mathbb{Q}_b$, internal nodes labeled $\{+,-,\times,\div\}$, size $L$ (number of leaves) and depth $D$.

Two distinct semantics must be told apart, because "the cost of computing $\mathrm{eval}(A)$" is not well-defined without doing so:

- **Rounded semantics** $\widetilde{\mathrm{eval}}(T)$: each operation is performed and rounded to $b$ bits (as real floating-point hardware does). This has cost $O(L)$ words, but the result can differ from the true value of the expression, and equality/order comparisons under this semantics are not always trustworthy near ties.
- **Exact semantics** $\mathrm{eval}_{\mathbb{Q}}(T) \in \mathbb{Q}$: every operation performed in exact rational arithmetic (no rounding). Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is a field, this is always well-defined when no division by zero occurs, but its bit-size need not stay bounded by $L\cdot b$.

**Proposition 1 (Exponential bit blow-up under nested division).** *There exist expression trees $T_n$ with $O(n)$ leaves of bit-length $O(1)$, depth $O(n)$, using only $\{+,\div\}$, such that the reduced numerator and denominator of $\mathrm{eval}_{\mathbb{Q}}(T_n)$ each require $\Omega(\phi^{\,n})$ bits, where $\phi = (1+\sqrt5)/2$.*

*Proof sketch.* Take $T_n$ to be the right-nested continued fraction $a_0 + \cfrac{1}{a_1 + \cfrac{1}{a_2 + \cdots + \cfrac{1}{a_n}}}$ with all $a_i \in \{1,2\}$ (so each $a_i$ costs $O(1)$ bits and the tree has $O(n)$ leaves and depth $O(n)$). The convergents $p_k/q_k$ of a continued fraction obey the continuant recurrence $q_k = a_k q_{k-1} + q_{k-2}$, $q_{-1}=0,\ q_0=1$. Since $a_k \ge 1$ for all $k$, this recurrence dominates the Fibonacci recurrence term-by-term, so $q_k \ge F_k = \Omega(\phi^{k})$ (this is the classical fact that continuants grow at least as fast as Fibonacci numbers; see e.g. Khinchin, *Continued Fractions*). Taking $k=n$ gives the claim; the numerator satisfies an identical bound by the same recurrence. $\blacksquare$

**Consequence.** The "classical baseline" of Section 4 — exact evaluation followed by comparison — does **not** have cost linear in $L$ in general; for division-heavy expressions it can be exponential in depth. This sharpens the original paper's cost claim and gives the search for $\mathbb{S}$ real headroom: beating a baseline that is sometimes exponential is a much lower bar than beating $O(L)$.

### 2.3 Requirement A1 — Comparability (revised)

The original A1 asserted $\le_{\mathbb S}(A,B) \iff \mathrm{eval}(A)\le\mathrm{eval}(B)$ "without the explicit full computation (in the average case, I guess)." As literally stated this is trivially satisfiable (take $\le_{\mathbb S} := \lambda A,B.\ \mathrm{eval}(A)\le\mathrm{eval}(B)$) and says nothing about cost. We replace it with a cost-indexed statement:

> **A1.** There is an algorithm computing $\le_{\mathbb S}(A,B)$ that is correct for **all** $A,B$ (worst case), and there is an instance parameter $g(A,B) \ge 0$ — a *gap* or *separation* measure, $g(A,B)=0$ iff $\mathrm{eval}(A)=\mathrm{eval}(B)$ — such that the algorithm's COST_ALL is a non-increasing function of $g(A,B)$, strictly below the classical baseline whenever $g(A,B) > \tau$ for some threshold $\tau$ depending on $b, L$.

This makes "average case" a precise, checkable statement (Theorems 2–3 exhibit exactly such a $g$ and $\tau$) instead of a hedge.

### 2.4 Requirement A2 — Structural distance (revised)

> **A2.** There is $d_{\mathbb S}:\mathbb S\times\mathbb S\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}$ and a **fixed**, $O(1)$-cost-to-evaluate, strictly increasing $\psi:\mathbb R_{\ge0}\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}$ such that $d_{\mathbb S}(A,B) = \psi(|\mathrm{eval}(A)-\mathrm{eval}(B)|)$ up to a *bounded, known distortion factor* $(1\pm\varepsilon)$, computable without full evaluation.

Requiring $\psi$ to be $O(1)$-cost and the distortion to be *known* rules out smuggling the hard part of the problem into $\psi$; it also makes $d_{\mathbb S}$ directly usable as the gap estimator $g$ in A1 — which is exactly the role it plays in Theorems 2 and 3 below.

### 2.5 Requirement A3 and the Isomorphism Lemma

The original A3 contained the correct intuition, stated confusingly: *"If it is simply isomorphic to $\mathbb R$ yet requires a lower COST_ALL, that is impossible."* We make this precise and prove it, since it is in fact a theorem, not an axiom:

**Lemma 1 (Isomorphism alone buys nothing).** *Let $\phi:\mathbb S\to\mathbb R$ be an order-isomorphism with $\phi = \mathrm{eval}$, and suppose the only procedure available for deciding $\le_{\mathbb S}(A,B)$ is "compute $\phi(A)$, compute $\phi(B)$, compare in $\mathbb R$." Then $\mathrm{COST\_ALL}_{\mathbb S} = \mathrm{COST\_ALL}_{\mathrm{baseline}} + O(1)$.*

*Proof.* Immediate: by hypothesis the procedure literally performs the baseline computation twice (once per operand) plus a single $O(1)$ real-number comparison. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary.** Any genuine reduction in COST_ALL must come from information about the *order relation* that is extractable from the structure of $A,B$ **without** fully inverting $\phi$ on both arguments — i.e., from a procedure that is not merely "evaluate-then-compare" wearing new notation. This is the operational content that A1 (§2.3) must have to be non-vacuous, and it is exactly what Theorems 2–3 supply for restricted instance classes.

With Lemma 1 in hand, A3 is simply: $\mathbb S$ has a total order at least as discriminating as $\mathbb R$ restricted to $\mathrm{eval}(\mathbb Q_b\text{-expressions})$, **and** some decision procedure for $\le_{\mathbb S}$ satisfies A1 non-vacuously (i.e., is not the procedure of Lemma 1).

### 2.6 COST_ALL and the Classical Baseline (formalized)

$$\mathrm{COST\_ALL}(A) \;=\; \alpha\cdot T(A) \;+\; \beta\cdot M(A) \;+\; \gamma\cdot C(A)$$

where $T(A)$ is word-RAM operation count, $M(A)$ is peak additional memory in bits beyond input storage, $C(A)$ is the number of memory-hierarchy block transfers (§2.1), and $\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0$ are machine-calibrated weights. COST_ALL is deliberately a **parametrized family**, not a single universal number: a claim "$\mathbb S$ beats the baseline" means the inequality holds asymptotically in $L$ (or $n$) for the regime of $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$ in which $T,M,C$ actually dominate wall-clock/energy cost on the target hardware — this is what "hardware-grounded" can mean without pretending a single constant captures every machine.

**Classical Baseline.** $T_{\mathrm{base}}(A) = \Theta(\text{bit-size of } \mathrm{eval}_{\mathbb Q}(A))$ for exact semantics (by Proposition 1, potentially exponential in depth), or $\Theta(L)$ for rounded semantics (but then correctness near ties is not guaranteed — this is precisely the accuracy/cost trade the rest of the paper is about).

## 3. Two Main Problems

**Main Problem 1 (General case).** $N$ expressions over $\{+,-,\times,\div\}$ with finite-precision leaves. Transcendental functions are excluded because, by Richardson's theorem (1968), deciding $\mathrm{eval}(A)=0$ is undecidable once they are permitted — so exclusion is forced by decidability, not a modeling choice.

**Main Problem 2 (Inner product).** $N$ expressions of the fixed form $\langle u,v\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n u_iv_i$, $u_i,v_i\in\mathbb Q_b$. Question: does the tighter structure make A1–A3 easier than in the general case?

## 4. Results

### 4.1 A universal lower bound

**Theorem 1 (Worst case cannot be beaten unconditionally).** *Any algorithm that correctly computes $\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{eval}_{\mathbb Q}(T))$ for every expression tree $T$ with $L$ leaves in $\mathbb Q_b$ must, in the worst case, probe $\Omega(Lb)$ input bits.*

*Proof.* Fix all leaves of $T$ except one, $x$, and set $T = x - c$ for a constant $c\in\mathbb Q_b$ chosen by the adversary. Let $x_1,x_2\in\mathbb Q_b$ be two values differing only in their least significant bit, with $c$ lying between them (such a triple exists by density of $\mathbb Q_b$ at resolution $2^{-b}$). Then $\mathrm{sign}(x_1-c)\ne\mathrm{sign}(x_2-c)$, but any algorithm that never probes the last bit of $x$ receives identical information on inputs $x_1,x_2$ and must therefore answer incorrectly on at least one. Repeating this argument on the last bit of every one of the $b$ bits of $x$, and on each of the $L$ leaves in turn (by relabeling which leaf plays the role of $x$), shows no bit of any leaf can be unconditionally skipped by a correct algorithm; hence $\Omega(Lb)$ bit-probes are necessary in the worst case. $\blacksquare$

**Interpretation.** This is a weak-sounding but important bound: it says no algorithm can be asymptotically cheaper than *reading the input*. It does **not** contradict the search for a cheaper $\mathbb S$, because by Proposition 1 the classical baseline can cost far more than reading the input ($\Omega(\phi^D)$ vs. $\Omega(Lb)$). Theorem 1 exactly delineates the target: the honest goal is to close the gap between $\Omega(Lb)$ (unavoidable) and $\Theta(\text{baseline})$ (Proposition 1), not to beat $\Omega(Lb)$ itself, which is impossible.

### 4.2 Main Problem 1, resolved on well-separated instances

**Theorem 2 (Adaptive-precision certified comparator; restatement of Shewchuk 1997 / Burnikel–Fleischer–Mehlhorn–Schirra in the COST_ALL framework).** *Define the separation $g(T) := |\mathrm{eval}_{\mathbb Q}(T)|$ when nonzero. There is an algorithm ADAPTIVE-SIGN that is correct for all $T$ (worst case) and runs in:*

- *$O(L)$ word-RAM operations whenever $g(T) > \varepsilon_{\mathrm{fp}}(T)$, where $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{fp}}(T)$ is a running forward-error bound on the $b$-bit rounded evaluation, computable in the same $O(L)$ pass;*
- *up to $\Theta(\text{bit-size of }\mathrm{eval}_{\mathbb Q}(T))$ (the classical baseline) in the worst case $g(T)\to 0$.*

*Proof sketch.* Compute $\hat v=\widetilde{\mathrm{eval}}(T)$ and a running error bound $\varepsilon$ by standard forward error analysis (each operation's rounding error is bounded by a known multiple of machine epsilon times an operand magnitude bound; see Higham, *Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms*). If $|\hat v|>\varepsilon$, the true value lies in $[\hat v-\varepsilon,\hat v+\varepsilon]$, an interval not containing $0$, so $\mathrm{sign}(\hat v)=\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{eval}_{\mathbb Q}(T))$ is *certified*, not guessed. Otherwise, double the working precision (double-double, quad, eventually exact bignum rationals) and repeat; termination is guaranteed because exact rational arithmetic has $\varepsilon=0$. Total cost is a geometric series dominated by the final (successful) round, so it is $O(1)\times$ the cost of the precision level at which separation is finally achieved — which, in the worst case, is the exact bit-size given by Proposition 1. $\blacksquare$

This gives A1 non-vacuously (§2.3, with $g$ as the gap parameter and $\tau = \varepsilon_{\mathrm{fp}}$) for the *generic* instance class — the technique already used in production for robust geometric predicates — while being honest that it degenerates exactly to the baseline on adversarial near-ties, consistent with Theorem 1.

### 4.3 Main Problem 2, resolved in the amortized query regime

A subtlety the original draft did not make explicit: sketching a **single** pair $(x,y)\in\mathbb Q_b^n\times\mathbb Q_b^n$ cannot beat $O(n)$, because computing the sketch $Rx$ itself costs $\Omega(n)$ per vector. The genuine saving in LSH/MIPS-style methods only appears in the **repeated-query** setting: one corpus of $N$ vectors, queried $Q$ times.

**Theorem 3 (Sketch-and-certify, amortized).** *Let $v_1,\dots,v_N,u_1,\dots,u_Q\in\mathbb Q_b^n$. Fix $\varepsilon,\delta_{\mathrm{fail}}>0$ and $k=O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log(N/\delta_{\mathrm{fail}}))$. Draw a Johnson–Lindenstrauss projection $R\in\mathbb R^{k\times n}$ (Johnson & Lindenstrauss 1984; Indyk & Motwani 1998). Then:*

1. *Preprocessing: compute $Rv_1,\dots,Rv_N$ once, cost $O(Nnk)$.*
2. *Per query $u_q$: compute $Ru_q$ ($O(nk)$), then for each $j$ estimate $\langle u_q,v_j\rangle$ via the polarization identity $\langle x,y\rangle=\tfrac14(\|x+y\|^2-\|x-y\|^2)$ applied to the sketches ($O(Nk)$ total), which by the JL guarantee is within additive error $O(\varepsilon\cdot\max(\|u_q\|^2,\|v_j\|^2))$ of the truth with probability $\ge 1-\delta_{\mathrm{fail}}$, simultaneously over all $N$ pairs (union bound).*
3. *Certify: for any pair whose estimated gap to a comparison threshold is below the error margin, fall back to exact evaluation ($O(n)$).*

*Total online cost per query is $O(nk+Nk+f\cdot n)$, where $f$ is the (data-dependent) number of near-tie pairs requiring fallback. This beats the baseline $O(Nn)$ per query whenever $k\ll n$ and $f\ll N$.*

*Proof.* Correctness of the sketch estimate follows from the JL lemma applied to $u_q+v_j$ and $u_q-v_j$ with a union bound over the $2N$ vectors involved per query batch, giving the stated failure probability. Certification on flagged pairs restores exactness (A3) exactly as in Theorem 2; uncertified pairs are, by construction, resolved by the same fallback. Cost accounting is as stated. $\blacksquare$

**This is the honest answer to Main Problem 2's open question.** The sum-of-products structure *does* make the problem easier than Main Problem 1 — but only once the right cost model (many queries against a fixed corpus) is used; on a single isolated comparison, Lemma 1's warning applies and no saving is possible in the worst case, matching Theorem 1.

**Meta-principle.** Theorems 2 and 3 are the same idea at two different structural levels: *approximate cheaply with a certified error bound, and pay the baseline cost only on the (hopefully rare) instances where the certificate fails.* Every genuine COST_ALL win found in this paper has this shape, and Theorem 1 shows this is not a coincidence — it is the only shape a win can take, since ties are provably where no bit can be skipped.

## 5. Status Reassessment

Revisiting the classification of Section 5 in the original draft with the results above:

- **Main Problem 1**, adversarial/worst case: **closed** (Theorem 1 — cannot be beaten below $\Omega(Lb)$; Proposition 1 shows the baseline itself can be much larger, so there is room, but not unlimited room).
- **Main Problem 1**, generic/well-separated case: **resolved in the affirmative** (Theorem 2), modulo citing the existing EGC literature — this was not a new discovery, but it is now stated as a theorem inside this paper's own framework rather than gestured at as a "precedent."
- **Main Problem 2**, single comparison, worst case: **closed**, same as Problem 1 (Theorem 1 applies verbatim with $L=n$).
- **Main Problem 2**, amortized/repeated-query, approximate: **resolved** (Theorem 3), with an explicit, checkable regime of applicability ($k\ll n$, few fallbacks).
- **Main Problem 2**, amortized, *exact* (A3 with zero failure probability): **still open**. Theorem 3 gives high-probability correctness, not certainty, for the pairs that are *not* flagged for fallback with a sufficiently generous margin; whether $f$ (the fallback count) can be bounded *deterministically* rather than in expectation over a data distribution is unresolved and is, honestly, closely related to open questions on data structures for exact nearest-neighbor search in high dimensions — where all known sublinear-query methods are approximate, and no proof of impossibility for an exact sublinear method is known to the author.

## 6. Avenues of Attack for the Adversarial Case, Corrected

Theorem 1 says the adversarial (near-tie) case cannot be beaten *unconditionally*. Sections 6.1–6.2 of the original draft speculated about structural, non-scalar semantics that might still help *conditionally* (e.g., by amortizing the certificate computation itself). Two corrections:

### 6.1 Tropical geometry — the subtraction gap

The max-plus semiring $(\mathbb R\cup\{-\infty\},\oplus,\otimes)$ with $x\oplus y=\max(x,y)$, $x\otimes y=x+y$ has **no additive inverse**: there is no tropical operation $\ominus$ with $x\oplus(\ominus x) = -\infty$ (the tropical zero) for general $x$, since $\max$ is idempotent ($x\oplus x = x$, not the tropical zero). This means naive tropicalization handles expressions built only from $\{+,\times\}$-analogues, **not** the $\{+,-,\times,\div\}$ expressions Main Problem 1 actually specifies — a gap the original draft did not flag. The relevant fix is the **symmetrized max-plus semiring** (signed tropical numbers / "the tropical hyperfield," developed to give max-plus algebra a notion of balance in place of subtraction; see Baccelli, Cohen, Olsder & Quadrat, *Synchronization and Linearity*, 1992, and later work by Akian, Gaubert & Guterman on tropical linear algebra), in which subtraction is replaced by a *balance relation* rather than an operation. Whether comparison in this symmetrized structure can be done at lower COST_ALL than exact evaluation is, itself, an open question this paper does not resolve — it is flagged here as the correct next object to study, replacing the original's unqualified "we can construct an extended tropical geometry."

### 6.2 Comparing operators — a classical, not only quantum, idea

The original draft's spectral/operator-algebra proposal is real but was framed as requiring quantum hardware, which undersells it: **on ordinary silicon**, two classical facts already let one bound eigenvalue-derived quantities without full diagonalization:

- **Weyl's inequality**: for Hermitian $A,B$ with eigenvalues sorted decreasingly, $|\lambda_i(A)-\lambda_i(B)|\le \|A-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ (Horn & Johnson, *Matrix Analysis*). A cheap operator-norm bound on $A-B$ therefore certifies an eigenvalue comparison without computing either spectrum exactly — the same certify-or-fall-back pattern as Theorems 2–3.
- **Randomized numerical linear algebra** (Halko, Martinsson & Tropp, *SIAM Review*, 2011) computes high-probability approximate eigenvalue/singular-value bounds using $O(k)$ matrix–vector products instead of a full $O(n^3)$ decomposition, for target rank $k\ll n$.

Combined, these give a genuinely classical (non-quantum) route to conditional wins in the spirit of Section 6.2's original intent, at the cost of narrowing its scope to instances where a low-rank or well-conditioned structure actually exists — which, as with Theorems 2–3, is exactly the price Theorem 1 says must be paid somewhere.

## 7. Limitations and Threats to Validity

Stated plainly, so the paper is not read as claiming more than it shows:

1. Theorem 2 is not a new result; it restates known exact-geometric-computation techniques inside this paper's cost model. Its contribution here is the restatement and the explicit $g,\tau$ characterization, not the underlying technique.
2. Theorem 3's failure probability is over the randomness of $R$, not over any assumption on the data; but the *number of fallback pairs* $f$ is a property of the data's gap distribution and is not bounded in this paper for worst-case data — only "few fallbacks $\Rightarrow$ a win" is shown, not "fallbacks are always few."
3. The claim in §5 that no sublinear *exact* nearest-neighbor method is known is a statement about the current state of the literature as best understood by the author, not a proven impossibility result; it should be read as "open and empirically resistant," not "settled."
4. Section 6's corrections identify the right mathematical objects (symmetrized tropical semirings, Weyl's inequality, randomized NLA) but do not prove that either avenue yields a COST_ALL win beyond what Theorem 3 already gives via a different route; they are flagged as promising directions, not additional theorems.
5. COST_ALL's weights $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$ are left uninstantiated; every "beats the baseline" claim in this paper is asymptotic and regime-dependent, not a single hardware-measured number.

## 8. Conclusion

The original question — is the scalar intermediate step necessary? — has a precise answer once "necessary" is given units: **no, not always, but the cases where a shortcut exists are exactly the non-adversarial ones, and this is provable rather than merely plausible** (Theorem 1 vs. Theorems 2–3). The interesting remaining mathematics is not "does $\mathbb S$ exist" in the abstract, but characterizing, for each structural class of expressions, how large the "hard core" of near-ties is and whether it can be shrunk — which is now a well-posed question rather than a philosophical one.
